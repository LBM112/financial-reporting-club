The Enron-Rhythms NetConnections Deal, Enron’s Insurance Against Itself

By Luca Basturkmen Massa · 27 November 2026 · 15 min read

Definitions At Bottom

Sounds crazy right? Betting on something to happen using yourself as collateral, however, during times of craze, bubbles, and potentially overreach and overconfidence, people start doing some properly crazy things. Enron was such a case, in the midst of the 1999 Dot-Com Boom, I have found myself intrigued in how it conducted one of its most interesting deals.

On April 7th, 1999, Enron found its $10,000,000 investment into pre-IPO shares (At $1.85) worth over $300,000,000 after a company named Rhythms NetConnections went public and reached an astounding $69 in one day of trading (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). The Dot-Com boom was in full swing, in what was the ‘biggest bull market ever seen in world history’ (up until that point) (McLean and Elkind, 2013), and understandably, when your bet pays off, but is still ongoing, you want to get out of it and lock in your gain as quickly as possible, right?

Enron desperately wished to lock-in its gain, as Enron was in dire need of profits to keep its stock price rising (for reasons that will later be revealed). However, there was a problem not many of us experience whilst using our betting apps, Enron was unable to sell its 5,400,000 shares (7% of company) of Rhythms due to a 270-day lockup agreement on its purchase (Unclear when exactly the lockup agreement ended, largely estimated to have been in November-December of 1999) (McLean and Elkind, 2013). Due to this inconvenience, Enron was unable to ‘lock-in’ its gain upon IPO as any commercial hedge (such as a put option or forward contract) would be prohibitively expensive or practically impossible (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002) due to:

  • Very high implied volatility due to Rhythms more than tripling in its opening day (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002)
  • Enron held 57.6% of Rhythms’ publicly traded float (McLean and Elkind, 2013)
  • In true Dot-Com fashion, Rhythms was a heavily loss-making DSL provider whose valuation rested almost entirely on hopes of future growth rather than any current profits

The effect on Enron’s valuation was especially concerning as Enron was using ‘Mark-To-Market' accounting, which in essence meant that assets on Enron’s balance sheet were valued based on their current market value (Healy and Palepu, 2003). This meant that Enron’s reported earnings would fluctuate violently if Rhythms NetConnections stock continued its volatility, as Enron would constantly have to mark the changed value of its interest in Rhythms, creating a risk-management danger of potentially substantial losses at inconvenient times. Alongside this, it would be a terrible look if its earnings dropped after already announcing the gains to investors. It would be similar to owning a baseball card whose value was dropping and rising so quickly due to outside speculation, and might just at any time go to zero, or at the moment you need cash or want to prove to your friends that you are a profitable baseball card trader. As a result, now that the card is at its highest value, you want to lock-in your gain today, although you inconveniently cannot sell the card due to a lock-up you had with the seller.

Before we advance into the legal structures of the deal, it is important to understand what truly was Enron, and why it decided to take this questionable route, and how it created a mindset at Enron of comfort with misleading finance, which later led to its collapse under a wave of accounting scandals in 2001 (Healy and Palepu, 2003).

Following the arrival of McKinsey advisor Jeffrey Skilling in 1990, Enron had transformed itself from an asset-heavy natural gas pipeline transporter from its inception in 1985, into an asset-light gas trading and originating business (Healy and Palepu, 2003; McLean and Elkind, 2013). This may sound simple, however in the 1990s this was an incredible business shift by a group of extraordinarily talented and smart individuals that had worked under Skilling (‘Skillingites’) (McLean and Elkind, 2013) to shoo away the cautious minded old heads of Texan natural gas into a new economy company that had created a market for something that had been too regulated to trade in such an aggressive manner. The investment community in New York was looking at Enron as an up-and-coming star of the new economy and saw Enron as an incredibly appealing investment into the future of trading. Skilling’s main idea (What he later called, his ‘Enchiladas’) was creating markets for things that had never been tradable as commodities before, and through creation, Enron would be the controller of the market and capture and significant enough portion of it to have seemingly arbitrary influence (McLean and Elkind, 2013).

Although Skilling was a genuine visionary and was a man seen as ‘incandescently brilliant’ (McLean and Elkind, 2013), these ideas had a problem: they swallowed up such enormous amounts of cash and human capital and did not consistently (or perhaps at all) create the incredible profits Skilling (and thus, investors) had envisioned. Skilling was always dogmatic in his view that these businesses would one day be successful through informational advantages for Enron, however, he was determined to avoid the image of his ideas being failures in the short-term, and this required profits to begin crystallising in these new businesses capturing new markets. Nobody had realised fully then, however that due to technology limitations, and the lack of any real market for some commodities, many of the aims for these new businesses ended up as impossible, without any economic basis for the incredible projections institutional analysts had been giving (Healy and Palepu, 2003).

Going back now to 1999, Enron set out its transaction like this:

  • Enron restructured a set of forward contracts it held with UBS to buy its own shares, which had risen sharply in value as Enron's stock climbed. This released 3,400,000 Enron shares (worth $276 million at the closing price on June 30th, 1999), which were then transferred to LJM1 (a partnership run and controlled by Enron's Chief Financial Officer, Andrew Fastow). These shares were, crucially, agreed to be transferred on the agreement that they could not be sold within four years (June 2003), and were discounted by 39% to $168 million to account for this. LJM1 gave Enron a note for $64 million due within 9 months in exchange for these shares (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002).
  • LJM1 transferred to ‘Swap-Sub' (A separate entity controlled to an extent, again, by Andrew Fastow) 1,600,000 Enron shares and $3,750,000 cash (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002)
  • Enron received from Swap-Sub, a put option on all 5,400,000 shares of Rhythms NetConnections for $56 per share for five years (June 2004), valued at $104 million (bringing note plus put-option at $168 million) (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002)

The idea was that Enron was tying its stake in Rhythms NetConnections to the value of its own stock (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002), which had been rising rapidly since 1997. This was because if the value of the restricted Enron shares owned by Swap-Sub and LJM1 rose, then Swap-Sub could make its obligations to Enron by taking out loans using Enron shares as collateral to purchase the Rhythms shares, and LJM1 could sell or take out loans in similar fashion to pay its $64 million note to Enron. This understandably would work (on paper) if the value of Enron shares continued to rise, or if Rhythms stood above its strike price of $56 until Enron unloaded its stake.

The interesting part of this deal, as you may have begun to realise, is that this is not any true or logical economic hedge that transferred risk to a non-related third party. It was a hedge of an Enron asset, backed by the value of Enron itself, and the assets were not independent of each other (because Swap-Sub could only pay if Enron's own share price held up) (Healy and Palepu, 2003; Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). Furthermore, there was the question of whether the entities themselves were even independent, and here is why:

Through a chain of general partner entities, Fastow controlled LJM1, which was a limited partner of Swap-Sub, and Swap-Sub’s general partner was LJMSwapCo, of whom Fastow was the sole director (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). This created a significant conflict of interest as Fastow was Enron’s Chief Financial Officer and was indirectly negotiating with Enron when the put option was created. As well as this, it is critical to understand that Swap-Sub was unconsolidated in Enron’s balance sheet because Swap-Sub required that it had 3% outside equity at risk (Somebody else needs to have a 3% invested, unrelated, at-risk investment in Swap-Sub) (Healy and Palepu, 2003; Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). This 3% was filled by LJM1... but LJM1 was an entity controlled by an Enron employee, creating an interesting question: Were the two entities technically independent of each other?

It is important to mention that LJM1 did have some outside investment within itself that qualified it as a non-related entity, because it had several investment banks (Credit Suisse and NatWest) who invested $15 million cash into it (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). But they were limited partners, and had no real day-to-day control over LJM1, meaning Fastow had effective control over almost all aspects of this deal, as he had significant power in his role as CFO and being General Partner of LJM. However, it is now crucial to question what the purpose of the rules even are? If LJM1 was accepted as non-consolidated although there was a substantial conflict of interest present (One where the Enron board had to waive its Code of Conduct with little information, for this deal to close) (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002), to what extent did the regulations for LJM1 keep up its intended purpose, of preventing misleading transactions like this from occurring?

Swap-Sub was in the end, falsely deemed non-consolidated, and the reason Arthur Andersen (The firm responsible for verifying Enron’s accounting) later accepted for this was that Swap-Sub held negative equity at inception. This is the case as its put option on Rhythms was valued on Enron’s books at 104 million, but Swap-Sub only held 84 million in assets (3.75 million cash, and 80 million in restricted Enron shares) giving it negative equity of ~20 million (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). Due to this, Swap-Sub failed to have 3% outside equity at risk and was erroneously approved to be non-consolidated. In normal circumstances, Swap-Sub would have been internalised in Enron's balance sheet, and the volatility of reporting profits would have continued to be problematic for Enron.

Going back to the question, would you bet on Haaland scoring 9 goals in two games in a row, with yourself as collateral after one game brought 9? In this case, you cannot find an insurance seller to cover this bet, and if Haaland fails next week, then your valuation gets wrecked by the loss of the value of the bet. Obviously, almost nobody would sell you any meaningful insurance on this bet, unless you paid them an astronomical premium (that would offset any payouts if a claim were made). Thus, to try to protect yourself from looking bad after telling all of your friends that Haaland is banging in 9 goals no problem, you sell insurance to yourself. But in this case, if Haaland goes dry, although it seems you have insurance, the ability for a payout rests on you, and you just watched your bet go to zero, and have no money to cover this, and so the insurance entity you created with your own equity is insolvent, and if not, at best, it only moves money from one pocket to another. Amazingly, at the time of these deals, Enron’s CFO Andrew Fastow was actually admired for these new ways of corporate finance and was even given the CFO Excellence Award for Capital Structure Management in 1999 (McLean and Elkind, 2013), and those same deals would end up becoming the case study of corporate financial ethics within two years, so what was the issue? The real reasons Rhythms was possible, and why it was allowed to close, was due to 3 main reasons:

  • Arthur Andersen (allegedly, according to speculation from the powers report) incorrectly valued Swap-Sub equity using the unrestricted value of the Enron shares it held, which was inappropriate due to the share’ restriction with LJM1 (Meaning that Swap-Sub held positive equity, and met the 3% equity rule) (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002).
  • GAAP rules during this time were narrow and failed to satisfy their intended purposes, and the 3% equity at risk requirement was largely arbitrary, and lack of regulatory oversight let the transaction be misstated until Enron restated it in November 2001 (Healy and Palepu, 2003).
  • The Enron Board of Directors knowingly allowed this transaction to close despite the enormous conflict of interest between Fastow and Enron (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002).

The Rhythms deal was wound down by March 2000, and the negotiations made the nature of the transaction even more bizarre. Firstly, an internal report within Enron in February had concluded that the deal had a 68% likelihood of failing due to inability to pay the obligations to Enron, so Enron decided to begin shutting it down (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). As negotiations (Between Enron and an Enron employee, Fastow) were underway, Enron provided an astonishing put option on its own shares to Swap-Sub, with a strike price of $71.31 when Enron was trading at $67.19, providing Swap Sub a free, immediate unrealised gain of $12.8 million to stabilise the entity as negotiations were ongoing (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002), which is unimaginable in almost any other circumstance, as Enron was providing free value to a company that owed Enron a derivative liability, symbolising the artificial nature of the transaction. To close the deal, Enron gave up its Rhythms options, valued at $207 million, plus about $27 million in cash. In return, Swap-Sub handed back 3.1 million Enron shares, worth $234 million at market value. On paper that looked even, but the shares were still restricted, and at their discounted value of about $161 million, Enron gave up more than $70 million more than it got back (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). What is even more astounding, is how the conflict of interest allowed Fastow (and many other finance staff at Enron) to negotiate themselves a huge windfall at Enron’s expense, as through a vehicle called ‘Southampton Place’, these staff members received returns of around 17,900% (Fastow himself, received a return of $4.5 million from a $25,000 investment, 17,900%) (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002). The Enron board were largely unaware of this, as they had been assured by Fastow that he would not profit personally from the transfer of Enron shares (Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002), but Enron’s management were willing to ignore such conflicts, as the transaction allowed Enron to lock in its gain in Rhythms through an artificial hedge, whilst Rhythms had fallen to $35 by the winddown.

Enron’s spectacular collapse will be explained in subsequent articles, however for the sake of the Rhythms deal here is what is relevant:

Due to a catastrophic collapse in investor and company confidence in late 2001, SEC investigations and key executive departures, Enron and Arthur Andersen acknowledged that many of its Special Purpose Entities had been incorrectly booked as non-consolidated (including Swap-Sub), and restated its financial statements from 1997 onwards, and consolidating Swap-Sub cut Enron's reported profit by $95 million in 1999 and $8 million in 2000 (Healy and Palepu, 2003; Powers, Troubh and Winokur, 2002), removing gains that had existed only because the 'hedge' sat off Enron's books, in other words, much of the profit Enron had shown in its financial statement, never existed. Enron fell into bankruptcy on December 2nd 2001 (Healy and Palepu, 2003). This remarkable transaction is a key lesson and insight into the fascinating SPE structures of Enron, and how they began contributing to the falsification of Enron’s health and why it collapsed so dramatically from October to December 2001.

Definitions:

Note: A formal, written agreement to repay a principal amount borrowed from another entity (A form of loan)

Hedge: The transferring of a financial risk to another party (Typically can be interpreted as insurance on an investment)

Put Option: A form of financial contract, where the buyer has the right to force the seller to purchase the agreed-upon underlying asset at a set price by a set date.

Commodity: A basic, tangible good that can be traded, such as agricultural product (Corn, Wheat, Livestock), minerals (Copper, Gold, Silver) and other raw materials (Timber, Water, Leather)

Consolidation: including an entity's assets, liabilities and profits in the parent company's own accounts, as if it were part of the company
