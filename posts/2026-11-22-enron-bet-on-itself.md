Would you bet on yourself that Haaland will score 9 goals next game?

Sounds crazy right? Betting on something to happen using yourself as collateral, however, during times of craze, bubbles, and potentially overreach and overconfidence, people start doing some properly crazy things. Enron was such a case, in the midst of the 1999 Dot-Com Boom, I have found myself intrigued in how it conducted one of its most interesting deals.

On April 7th, 1999, Enron found its $10,000,000 investment into pre-IPO shares (At $1.85) worth over $300,000,000 after a company named Rhythms NetConnections went public and reached an astounding $69 in one day of trading. The Dot-Com boom was in full swing, in what was the ‘biggest bull market ever seen in world history’ (up until that point), and understandably, when your bet pays off, but is still ongoing, you want to get out of it and cash out as quickly as possible, right?

Enron desperately wished to lock-in its gain, as Enron was in dire need of profits to keep its stock price rising (for reasons that will later be revealed). However, there was a problem not many of us experience whilst using our betting apps, Enron was unable to sell its 5,400,000 shares (7% of company) of Rhythms due to a 270-day lockup agreement on its purchase (Unclear when exactly the lockup agreement ended, largely estimated to have been in November-December of 1999). Due to this inconvenience, Enron was unable to ‘lock-in’ its gain upon IPO as any commercial hedge (such as a put option or forward contract) would be prohibitively expensive or practically impossible due to:

1. Very high implied volatility due to Rhythms more than tripling in its opening day

2. Enron held 57.6% of Rhythms’ publicly traded float

3. In true Dot-Com fashion, Rhythms was a heavily loss-making DSL provider whose valuation rested almost entirely on hopes of future growth rather than any current profits

The effect on Enron’s valuation was especially concerning as Enron was using ‘Mark-To-Market' accounting, where in essence, meant that assets on Enron’s balance sheet were valued based on their current market value. This meant that the Enron’s earnings sheet would fluctuate violently if Rhythms NetConnections stock continued its volatility, as Enron would constantly have to mark the changed value its interest in Rhythms, creating a risk-management danger of potentially substantial losses at inconvenient times. Alongside this, it would be a terrible look if its earnings dropped after already announcing the gains to investors. It would be similar to owning a baseball card whose value was dropping and rising so quickly due to outside speculation, and might just at any time go to zero, or at the moment you need cash or want to prove to your friends that you are a profitable baseball card trader. As a result, now that the card is at its highest value, you want to lock-in your gain today, although you inconveniently cannot sell the card due to a lock-up you had with the seller.

Before we advance into the legal structures of the deal, it is important to understand what truly was Enron, and why it decided to take this questionable route, and how it created a mindset at Enron of comfort with misleading finance, which later led to its collapse under a wave of accounting scandals in 2001.

Following the arrival of McKinsey advisor Jeffery Skilling in 1990, Enron had transformed itself from an asset-heavy natural gas pipeline transporter from its inception in 1985, into an asset-light gas trading and originating business. This may sound simple, however in the 1990s this was an incredible business shift by a group of extraordinarily talented and smart individuals that had worked under Skilling (‘Skillingites’) to shew away the cautious minded old heads of Texan natural gas into a new economy company that had created a market for something that had been too regulated to trade in such an aggressive manner. The investment community in New York was looking at Enron as an up-and-coming star of the new economy and saw Enron as an incredibly appealing investment into the future of trading. Skilling’s main idea (What he later called, his ‘Enchiladas’) was creating markets for things that had never been tradable as commodities before, and through creation, Enron would be the controller of the market and capture and significant enough portion of it to have seemingly arbitrary influence.

Although Skilling was a genuine visionary and was a man seen as ‘incandescently brilliant’, these ideas had a problem: they swallowed up such enormous amounts of cash and human capital and did not consistently (or perhaps at all) create the incredible profits Skilling (and thus, investors) had envisioned. Skilling, determined to avoid the image of his ideas being failures, required profits to be shown in these new businesses capturing these markets to begin crystallising, or at least, seem to be crystallising. Nobody had realised fully then, however that due to technology limiations, and the lack of any real market for some commodities, many of these projects ended up as White elephants without any economic basis for the incredible projections institutional analysts had been giving.

Going back now to 1999, Enron set out its transaction like this:

1. Enron restructured a set of forward contracts it held with UBS to buy its own shares, which had risen sharply in value as Enron's stock climbed. This released 3,400,000 Enron shares (worth $276 million at the closing price on June 30th, 1999), which were then transferred to LJM1, a partnership run and controlled by Enron's Chief Financial Officer, Andrew Fastow. These shares were, crucially, agreed to be transferred on the agreement that they could not be sold within four years (June 2003), and were discounted by 39% to $168 million to account for this. LJM gave Enron a note for $64 million due within 9 months in exchange for these shares.

2. LJM1 transferred to ‘Swap-Sub' (A separate entity controlled to an extent, again, by Andrew Fastow) 1,600,000 Enron shares and $3,750,000 cash

3. Enron received from Swap-Sub, a put option on all 5,400,000 shares of Rhythms NetConnections for $56 per share for five years (June 2004), valued at $104 million (bringing note plus put-option at $168 million)

The idea was that Enron was tying its stake in Rhythms NetConnections to the value of its own stock, which had been rising rapidly since 1997. This was because if the value of the restricted Enron shares owned by Swap-Sub and LJM1 rose, then Swap-Sub could make its obligations to Enron by taking out loans using Enron shares as collateral to purchase the Rhythms shares, and LJM1 could sell or take out loans in similar fashion to pay its $64 million note to Enron. This understandably would work if the value of Enron shares continued to rise, or if Rhythms stood above its strike price of $56 until Enron unloaded its stake.

The interesting part of this deal, as you may have begun to realise, is that this is not any true or logical economic hedge that transferred risk to a non-related third party. It was a hedge of an Enron asset, backed by the value of Enron itself, and the assets were not independent of each other. Furthermore, there was the question of whether the entities themselves were even independent, and here is why:

Through a chain of general partner entities, Fastow controlled LJM1, which was the sole director of Swap-Sub. This created a significant conflict of interest as Fastow was Enron’s Chief Financial Officer and was indirectly negotiating with Enron when the put option was created. As well as this, it is critical to understand that Swap-Sub was unconsolidated in Enron’s balance sheet because Swap-Sub required that it had 3% outside equity at risk (Somebody else needs to have a 3% invested, unrelated, at-risk investment in Swap-Sub). This 3% was filled by LJM1... but LJM1 was an entity controlled by an Enron employee,

Now, ignoring this legal structure, LJM1 did have some outside investment within itself that qualified it as a non-related entity, because it had several investment banks (Credit Suisse and NatWest) who invested $15 million cash into it, but were Limited Parters, so had no real day-to-day control over LJM1, however it is now crucial to question what the purpose of the rules even are? If LJM1 was accepted as non-consolidated although there was a substantial conflict of interest present (One where Enron had to waive its Code of Conduct for this deal to close), to what extent did the regulations for LJM1 keep up its intended purpose, of preventing misleading transactions like this from occurring.

Swap-Sub was in the end, falsely deemed non-consolidated, and the reason Arthur Andersen (The firm responsible for verifying Enron’s accounting) later accepted for this was simple: it held negative equity at inception. This is the case as it s put option on Rhythms was valued on Enron’s books at 104 million, but Swap-Sub only held ~84 million in assets (3.75 million cash, and ~80 million in restricted Enron shares) giving it negative equity of ~20 million. This fact alone is why it was later deemed as inappropriate to be considered non-consolidated. In the end, this transaction was never a true hedge because in normal circumstances, Swap-Sub would have been internal in Enron's balance sheet, and the volatility of reporting profits would have been continued, defeating the entire purpose of a hedge.
